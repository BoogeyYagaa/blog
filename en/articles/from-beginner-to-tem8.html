From Beginner to TEM-8: Three Habits That Changed Everything

July 22, 2026 · 5 min read

I was the weakest English student in my class when I started university. The placement test put me in the lowest level. I understood less than a third of what was said in listening class. My writing was a patchwork of high school template sentences.

Four years later, I passed TEM-8 (Test for English Majors — Band 8) with distinction. Looking back, the biggest turning point wasn't any "breakthrough method" — it was three simple habit changes.

Habit 1: Stop memorizing vocabulary. Start contextual input.

In my freshman spring, I made a decision: no more isolated vocabulary drilling. The reason was practical — I forgot as fast as I memorized, and I couldn't use the words I learned. Encountering a word seven times in different contexts beats copying it twenty times from a word list.

My approach: read one article slightly above my level every day. Don't look up every unknown word — only those that keep appearing and block comprehension. Three passes: first for gist, second for key words, third for sentence structure review.

After three months, my reading speed doubled. More importantly, I began to "think" in English — understanding sentences directly without first translating them into Chinese.

Habit 2: The i+1 material selection rule

Most struggling learners fall into one of two traps: reading only simple content (no progress) or jumping straight to native-level materials (too hard to sustain).

My rule was i+1: i being my current level, +1 being just slightly harder. The test: read a paragraph. If you understand 70-80% of the main idea but need to look up 5-10 words to fully grasp it, you're in the right zone. My progression: Oxford Bookworms graded readers → VOA Learning English → The Economist Espresso → full-length non-fiction and long-form journalism.

Habit 3: Comparative output practice

High input volume doesn't automatically produce strong output. For TEM-8, I needed writing and translation skills too. My most effective technique was comparative output: translate a passage myself, then compare it with a reference translation. The goal wasn't to copy, but to identify gaps — which collocations did I miss? Why is the reference version more concise? Am I being led by the Chinese sentence structure?

Once a week, for six months. The result: my writing shifted from "grammatically correct but unnatural" to "closer to native expression."
