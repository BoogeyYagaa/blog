My English Learning Story

This is for anyone who thinks they're "just not good at languages."

Starting Point: Barely Passing

When I entered university, my English placement score was the lowest in my class. I had scraped through the Gaokao English exam with a borderline passing score. My vocabulary was well under 4,000 words. In listening class, I understood less than a third of what was said.

Turning Point: From Vocabulary Lists to Reading

Freshman year, I made a decision that seemed risky at the time: I stopped drilling vocabulary lists entirely. Every word I had memorized in isolation, I forgot. Every word I encountered in a meaningful article, I retained.

My new strategy was simple: read one article slightly above my level every day. Not to memorize words, but to understand ideas. One article, three passes: first for gist, second for key words, third for sentence structure.

Three months later, my reading speed had doubled. More importantly, I stopped translating in my head.

Climbing: Building Intuition Through Input Volume

I maintained at least one hour of English input daily throughout my sophomore and junior years. Reading: from graded readers to The Economist and The New Yorker. Listening: from BBC slow English to NPR broadcasts and YouTube without subtitles. Writing: a 300-word journal entry each week, prioritizing having something to say over grammatical perfection.

The change was not linear. For weeks, I felt no progress. Then one day, I realized I understood a BBC broadcast without pausing. These moments of sudden clarity are the hallmark of language acquisition.

TEM-8: The Natural Result

When it came time to take TEM-8 (Test for English Majors — Band 8), I barely prepared. No grammar drills, no vocabulary cramming. I did two practice tests to familiarize myself with the format. That was it. Result: Distinction. The foundation had been built over two years of consistent input.

Translation Master's: From Understanding to Expression

Now pursuing a Master's in Translation at Guangdong University of Technology, I face a new challenge — moving from "I know what this means" to "I can make it clear to others." My most effective technique here has been comparative output: translate a passage independently, then compare it to a reference translation. Not to imitate, but to see what I consistently miss.

My Timeline

  • 2021

  • 2022-2023
